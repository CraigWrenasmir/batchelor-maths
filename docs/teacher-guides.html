Teacher guides

Five investigations for modelling on the Smartboard and individual or paired practice on an iPad. Choose one activity for a first trial. These are practice opportunities, not assessments. Challenge, support and age are separate: an older learner may benefit from small numbers with visible prompts, and a confident learner may need a demanding task with help using the device.

The school scope-and-sequence attachments contained no readable content, so school alignment remains to be checked. The provisional curriculum map identifies selected Australian Curriculum v9 connections. Year levels and codes support teacher planning; they are not student labels. A possible 15–20-minute session

This timing is a suggested trial plan, not a claim about sustained engagement. Shorten, pause, repeat or move to physical materials when useful. Have counters, blocks, paper strips or a paper grid ready.

TimeWhat to do
3 minutes · ModelUse Big screen mode. Demonstrate the controls with Show me, then solve one small example together. Think aloud briefly and let a student demonstrate the next action.
5–7 minutes · TryStudents try a related task individually or in pairs. In pairs, one plans and the other operates the controls; swap roles. Notice strategies before adding prompts.
4–6 minutes · Explain and changeCompare two approaches. Change a quantity, landmark, pattern step or route condition. Predict, test and revise. Invite a partner challenge when the activity offers it.
3–4 minutes · Check away from the screenAsk students to show the same idea with objects, a paper line, a pattern or arrow cards. Accept gesture, moving objects, speech or a student's usual communication system.

Keep the mathematical task similar when moving from board demonstration to iPad practice. Establish unfamiliar words through action. Successful taps alone do not establish understanding: distinguish device, language, attention and memory demands from the mathematics.

Use school-approved contexts. Ask students what they would like to count, pack or navigate before developing later local versions. Local stories, language and cultural imagery need appropriate school and community contributions.

How many?

Focus and starting point

Estimate a collection, find its total, and explain a useful grouping. Start with a quantity students can count accurately. Use groups of five or ten when students are ready to treat a group as a unit; use arrays to connect rows with multiplication.

Selected connections: AC9MFN01–03; AC9M1N01–03; AC9M1A01; AC9M2N02, AC9M2N05; AC9M3N04; AC9M4A02. Preset limits and partial coverage are described in the curriculum map.

Model

  1. Ask for an estimate and enter it, or choose Skip the estimate to focus on counting. An estimate is a useful starting idea, not a score.
  2. Demonstrate one-to-one counting by tapping objects. For a scattered collection, choose Make groups of 5/10; the same objects are rearranged.
  3. Use a whole-group or row button to count that group. If some members were already counted, the button adds only those left. Undo reverses the last counting action.
  4. If the class has worked out the total by grouping or multiplication, choose I know the total and enter it. A wrong answer gives a direction to investigate and can be retried; it does not reveal the total.
  5. Compare the final total with the estimate and discuss the checking strategy. Try the original and grouped layouts: “What changed? Did the total change?”

Adjust support and challenge

  • For students losing track, start with a small collection, visible tally and physical counters alongside it. Count one remains an alternative to small on-screen objects.
  • To deepen reasoning without larger numbers, ask for two ways to check the same total or a prediction after adding five.
  • For arrays, ask how many are in one row and how many rows there are before entering a total. Whole-row buttons can verify the reasoning without tapping every object.
  • Quick look is optional. The “Up to 10” preset can show more than five objects, so use small groups or teacher-selected physical collections for focused subitising to five. Keep the scene visible when time pressure adds an access barrier.

Check away from the screen

Give a collection matched to the student's range. Ask for an estimate and a way to count without losing track. Rearrange it and ask whether the total changed. Listen for groups and leftovers, rather than requiring a written equation.

Where does it go?

Focus and starting point

Place numbers using size and landmarks: ends, halfway, tens or whole numbers. Start with a range and representation the learner recognises. Fraction, decimal and integer presets extend the mathematical content while retaining the same interface.

Selected connections: AC9MFN01, AC9M1N01, AC9M2N01, AC9M3N01; AC9M2N03, AC9M3N02, AC9M5N03, AC9M6N03; AC9M4N01, AC9M5N01; AC9M6N01.

Model

  1. Point to the ends and a useful landmark. Ask where a card belongs and why before placing it.
  2. Select a card, then tap the line. Use Move left and Move right, or the keyboard arrows, to refine the position.
  3. Press Check. Close and exactly placed are different results. Close cards stay editable; use the feedback and landmarks to adjust and check again.
  4. Ask a partner to explain the placement: “It is past halfway”, “It is between 10 and 20”, or “It is one quarter of the way along.”

Adjust support and challenge

  • Begin with visible ticks and smaller ranges. Move to fewer landmarks when students can explain how they locate an unmarked value.
  • For fractions, fold a paper strip into equal parts alongside the line. Explicitly connect the parts to the labels.
  • In Mystery number, use bigger/smaller clues to discuss which values remain possible. Compare two guessing strategies; a small guess count is a discussion point, not a leaderboard or a test of speed.
  • The road-distance preset contains draft local figures. Check distances and starting points with the school before teaching them as facts; use ordinary number presets in the meantime.

Check away from the screen

Draw a line with the same endpoints on paper. Ask the student to place three number cards and explain the landmarks used. Allow an approximate physical placement followed by a precise verbal or written value.

Grow the pattern

Focus and starting point

For repeating patterns, find and continue the repeating unit. For growing patterns, build the next step, compare its shape and total, predict later totals and explain the rule. Begin with copying a simple pattern or counting a small array before introducing distant predictions.

Selected connections: AC9MFA01; AC9M1A01, AC9M2A01, AC9M3A01; AC9M6A01, AC9M7A01. The last two are opportunities for guided generalisation, not a complete algebra programme.

Model

  1. Look at the first three steps. Ask what stays the same and what changes.
  2. Build step 4 on the larger tap grid, then check both the number of tiles and the arrangement.
  3. If the total matches but the shape differs, compare the learner's construction with the shown pattern. The learner's work stays visible. They can revise it or keep it as an alternative.
  4. When predicting later totals, use the growth rule in the shown pattern. A different arrangement with the same total does not by itself establish the same growth rule.
  5. For stairs, identify the new column at each step: the amount added grows by one each time. This is different from a pattern that adds a constant number of tiles.

Adjust support and challenge

  • Say, clap or build a repeating unit with physical objects before continuing it on screen.
  • Keep numbers manageable and ask students to identify the fixed part and the growing part. Accept a demonstration before asking for a symbolic rule.
  • Use step 10 or 100 to invite generalisation rather than drawing every step. Compare a student's strategy with the displayed rule.
  • A learner may choose an arrangement that looks different but follows a defensible rule. Discuss that rule separately; the app's later questions still refer to the supplied pattern.

Check away from the screen

Show three steps made with blocks. Ask the student to build step 4, then predict step 6 without building it. Invite them to make a pattern for a partner and explain how it changes.

Pack the gear

Focus and starting point

Make a collection, see it as two parts, keep track of its total and reason about a changed amount. Choose amounts up to 5, 10 or 20. Start small enough that the learner can attend to relationships between the parts and the whole.

Selected connections: AC9MFN04 (partition and combine up to 10); AC9M1N04 (addition and subtraction within 20).

Model and explore the five parts

  1. Make the request: use the tray + and − buttons to make the requested total. Ask how the learner knows the total.
  2. Find another split: move one object between trays using A → B or B → A. Ask what changed and what stayed the same.
  3. Change the request: adjust the existing collection rather than starting over. Predict how many need adding or removing.
  4. Repair: inspect a collection with too many or too few and show a useful change.
  5. Make a challenge: use the request + and − controls to choose a target within the selected range. A partner can pack it and find another split.

Accept every valid partition, including an empty tray. A different split means a different distribution between trays, not moving objects within one tray. Dwell on a useful part rather than treating completion of all five as the goal.

Adjust support and challenge

  • Visible prompts: request dots, numbered objects, a running equation and feedback about how many more or fewer are needed.
  • Hints on request: the collections, tray counts and running equation remain visible; learners can request a hint.
  • Try independently: collections and tray counts remain visible; the equation appears after checking. Examples and hints remain available.
  • Increase reasoning without increasing the range: predict whether transferring one object changes the total, or ask for a second way to make the same request. A written equation is not required to demonstrate understanding.

Check away from the screen

Make six objects in two groups. Move one object between groups and ask whether there are still six. Ask the student to show another split, then change the total by one.

Find the way

Focus and starting point

Give, follow, test and revise directions that move an object to a location. First check that the learner can match one arrow to one square of movement. Short routes use a 4 × 4 grid; More steps uses 5 × 5; Changing routes uses 5 × 5 with a round stop that must be visited before the flag.

Selected connection: AC9M1SP02 (give and follow directions to relocate people or objects within a space).

Model and explore the four parts

  1. Plan: trace a route, enter arrows and predict where it ends before choosing Test route.
  2. Change: an extra block appears and the previous arrow sequence remains. Test and revise it around the block.
  3. Repair: inspect a supplied sequence. Locate the first instruction that needs changing.
  4. Make a challenge: choose Choose flag position, then an available square. A partner plans a route to the new destination. The start, blocks and any round stop stay in place.

The arrows mean up, down, left and right on the displayed grid, one square at a time. They do not mean turning left or right, compass bearings, or forward relative to an object's facing direction. Demonstrate this distinction when transferring to physical movement.

Tap an arrow in the sequence to remove it; Undo last removes the final arrow. Every test begins at the start. A boundary or block gives information for revision. Accept any permitted route that ends at the flag and visits the round stop when shown; the shortest route is an optional discussion, not the only correct route. Sequences allow up to 18 arrows for Short routes and 28 for the other settings.

Adjust support and challenge

Visible prompts give fuller reminders to connect arrows and squares. Hints on request and Try independently use the same shorter persistent prompts; the latter signals an initial independent attempt. Hints and examples remain available in every setting. In a pair, one learner plans and the other follows or enters the directions, then swap.

Check away from the screen

Place an object on a paper grid in the same orientation. Use arrow cards to direct it. Later explore directions in a familiar physical space with adult guidance, explicitly teaching changes in viewpoint and directional language.

Controls, support and the first trial

  • Listen reads the current instruction on request. Show me opens a separate example and preserves current work. Read feedback optionally reads responses as they appear. Speech uses the browser/device voice; preview it on classroom equipment. It does not interpret student speech or provide literacy teaching.
  • Big screen enlarges the presentation and adds teacher controls. Available controls vary by activity. Revealing a solution is supported practice, not evidence of independent understanding.
  • New learner clears work and starts a fresh round or session. For Pack/Find, Start again resets the current part. Applying setup also starts fresh.
  • Round code plus the same activity and settings gives matching starting tasks on devices. Links do not contain responses or current progress. Pack has three number sequences per range; Find has four reflected map variants per route setting. These repeat; final stages allow student-created challenges.
  • Full screen is optional and depends on the device. Adding a Home Screen shortcut does not guarantee offline access. Test the actual school browser, network and board before the first session.
  • Keyboard and touch: use Tab and visible buttons; number-line adjustment controls avoid relying on precise dragging. All activities provide button/tap actions. Physical-device and assistive-technology checks remain part of the classroom trial.

What to notice

Observe which supports make the controls and language accessible; whether students count, group, predict, compare or revise deliberately; when they seek help; and whether the idea transfers to objects or paper. Note whether they return to a problem or create a challenge, without treating speed as success. Ask students what they would change.

No performance history or student details are saved by the sampler. Teachers can record brief observations through their usual school process. Readable school curriculum documents and the first trial should guide the next content and support choices.
