Teacher guides

Three investigations, each built to run for 15 to 20 minutes with talk, not a 30-second tap drill. Each guide gives the objective, what students need first, a demonstration, a routine, how to adjust support and challenge, and a quick check away from the screen. Year levels and curriculum codes are listed here for planning only; the student screen never shows them.

How many?

Objective

Students estimate the size of a collection, then count it accurately by organising it into groups (fives, tens, rows), and compare the count with their estimate. They explain the structure: "2 tens and 3 ones", "4 rows of 6", "3 groups of 5 and 2 more".

Australian Curriculum v9: AC9MFN02, AC9MFN03 (subitise, quantify and compare to 20); AC9M1N01, AC9M1N02, AC9M1N03 (to 120, partition into tens and ones, skip count); AC9M2N02, AC9M2N05 (regroup, arrays); AC9M3N04, AC9M4A02 (multiplication with arrays).

Before you start

  • Students can count a small collection by touching each item once (one-to-one).
  • For presets with tens: students have met "ten" as a unit (a bundle, a ten-frame, a rod).
  • For arrays: students have counted equal rows before and can skip count by 2, 5 or 10.

Demonstration (5 minutes, big screen)

  1. Open the activity in Big screen mode with a preset such as "Up to 50, scattered, count in tens" and a context the class likes (termite mounds, fish, mangoes).
  2. Ask the class for estimates. Take three or four, including an unlikely one. Type one into the keypad. Say: "We will find out how close we were."
  3. Tap items to count. Point out the colour change every ten and the rods building up in the tally. Say the tally aloud: "2 tens and 3 is 23".
  4. Tap "I have counted them all" and read the feedback together. Ask: "Was our estimate more or fewer? By how many?"
  5. Use Hint before the next round to show what a group of ten looks like, then ask for estimates again.

Suggested routine (15 to 20 minutes, iPads in pairs)

  1. Pairs take turns: one estimates, the other counts, then swap.
  2. After each round, pairs tell each other one thing that helped the estimate.
  3. After four rounds, bring the class back to the board for one shared round and the talk prompts.

Check away from the screen

Put a handful of counters, bottle tops or stones on the table (between 12 and 40, matched to the preset). Ask for an estimate, then ask the student to count them "in a way that helps you not lose track". Note whether they group, and whether they can say the total as groups and leftovers ("3 tens and 4").

Where does it go?

Objective

Students reason about the size of numbers by placing them on an empty or lightly marked number line, using landmarks (0, halfway, tens) to justify the position. In Mystery number, they use "bigger / smaller" clues and see the possible range shrink, which builds the halving strategy.

Australian Curriculum v9: AC9MFN01, AC9M1N01, AC9M2N01, AC9M3N01 (represent and order numbers to 20, 120, 1000 and beyond on number lines); AC9M2N03, AC9M3N02, AC9M5N03, AC9M6N03 (fractions on a number line); AC9M4N01 (decimals); AC9M6N01 (integers).

Before you start

  • Students can read the numbers in the preset range (or the dot cards in 0 to 20).
  • Students have used a numbered line or hundred chart before. The empty line is a step on from that.

Demonstration (5 minutes, big screen)

  1. Open "0 to 100" in Big screen mode. Point to the ends and the halfway mark. Ask: "What number lives here?"
  2. Pick a card. Ask three students where it should go and why, before anyone touches the board. Place it where the class agrees.
  3. Place the rest, then press Check. Dashed markers are not right yet: the feedback says which way to move them. Move them and check again. The green triangles then show the exact spot.
  4. Read the feedback sentence aloud: "17 is between 10 and 20, closer to 20." This is the language you want students to use.

Suggested routine

  1. Pairs on iPads: one places, the other has to agree before Check. Two rounds.
  2. Switch to Mystery number. Challenge: find it in the fewest guesses. Pairs record their guess counts on paper and discuss what a "good" first guess is.
  3. Back on the board: use Hint in Mystery mode to make the halving strategy explicit.

Adjusting support and challenge

If...Try
Students place everything at one endUse a preset with tick marks (0 to 100) and Full support, and talk about halfway first.
Students are confident with whole numbers"0 to 100, no tick marks", then "0 to 1000", then fractions: halves and quarters.
Fraction placement is randomGo back to "halves and quarters" and fold a strip of paper to match.

Older students, integers or decimals"Integers -10 to 10" and "Decimals 0 to 10". Same screen, no change in look.

Check away from the screen

Draw a line on paper with 0 at one end and 100 (or the preset's top number) at the other. Give three numbers on cards. Ask the student to place them and say why. Listen for landmarks: "it's past halfway", "it's close to 100".

Grow the pattern

Objective

Students see how a visual pattern grows, build the next step, predict a step they cannot see, and describe the rule in words ("each step adds 2, plus the corner"). This is early algebraic thinking: a relationship between the step number and the number of tiles. The repeating-pattern presets build the earlier skill of recognising a unit that repeats.

Australian Curriculum v9: AC9MFA01 (repeating patterns); AC9M1A01, AC9M2A01 (skip counting and additive patterns that increase by a constant); AC9M3A01 (unknown values); AC9M6A01 (rules for visually growing patterns); AC9M7A01 (variables and formulas).

Before you start

  • Repeating patterns: students can copy a simple two-colour pattern with objects.
  • Growing patterns: students can count a small array and add on a small number.
  • Find the rule: students can multiply by a one-digit number.

Demonstration (5 minutes, big screen)

  1. Open "Growing patterns with a fixed part". Ask: "What is the same in every step? What is different?"
  2. Count the tiles in steps 1, 2 and 3 together. Ask: "How many more each time?"
  3. Invite a student to build step 4 on the board by tapping squares. Check. The feedback colours the fixed tiles blue and the added tiles orange.
  4. Ask for step 5 without building it. Then step 10. Use the table of values. Ask: "How could we work out step 100?"

Suggested routine

  1. Pairs on iPads: build, predict, read the rule. Two patterns.
  2. Away from the screen: pairs make their own growing pattern with blocks or counters and give it to another pair to continue.
  3. Class share: one pattern on the board, class predicts step 10 together.

Adjusting support and challenge

If...Try
Students cannot see what repeatsRepeating patterns preset. Say the pattern aloud and clap it. Use Hint to show the unit.
Students build the right count but a different shapeThat is fine: Check judges the count. Ask them to explain where the new tiles went.
Students predict by drawing every stepAsk for step 100. "Find the rule" preset adds step 100 to the table.
Students want something harder"Challenge: stairs" grows by a different amount each step.

Check away from the screen

Show three steps of a pattern made with blocks (for example an L shape). Ask the student to build step 4, then tell you how many blocks step 6 would need without building it, and how they know.

Whole-class routines and controls

  • Big screen mode makes everything larger and adds a teacher bar: Hint (H), Reveal (R), Next (N), Start again (S). The letters work on a keyboard; the buttons work by touch.
  • Sound is off until someone turns it on. It uses the device's own voice to read numbers and feedback. If the device has no voice, the button is greyed out and nothing else changes.
  • New learner clears the current round so the next student starts fresh. Nothing about a student is saved.
  • Round code (in Set up) makes every iPad show the same rounds, which is useful for a shared discussion.
  • Full screen is optional. On iPad, add the page to the Home Screen for a cleaner view.
  • Keyboard: every control is reachable with Tab. On the number line, arrow keys move the marker and Enter confirms. In How many?, "Count next" counts without needing to tap each item.
